This is the tale of my third installment of wild Scottish solo hiking. Last year, I read a wonderful book called [Lonely Hills & Wilderness Trails](https://archive.org/details/lonelyhillswilde0000gilb) in which the author, Richard Gilbert, describes a 4-day hike he did with his wife traversing the majority of the Cairngorms from North to South. He mentions getting rained on the entire time, which really sealed the deal for me. I dutifully plotted the route as per his description, before sprinkling in some extras to keep things interesting, and then set off in the trusty Yaris in the wee hours of Good Friday!

My plan was to start in Blair Atholl, then hike northwards through Glen Tilt, taking a small detour to visit a bothy for the first night. This glen then pops out near the foot of the Cairngorms mountains themselves. There is ample camping for the second night in this area, plus another bothy. At this point, I could either climb Devil's Point to the west and walk all along the famous ridge (bagging some munroes as I go!) before dropping down into Glenmore for the third night. Alternatively, if conditions are not favourable, the Lairig Ghru valley runs alongside the ridge at a lower altitude and ends up in the same spot. From Glenmore, it is a short walk to Aviemore where I planned to get the train back to Blair Atholl to complete the loop. The only thing remaining was impeccable weather which I surely deserved after concocting such a wonderful plan.

## Day 1: Blair Atholl to Tarf Hotel

The drive up north was nice but some inclement weather en route gave me the jitters. I also noticed a healthy covering of snow still present on the tops of the hills, which made me wonder if I should have packed my microspikes for the ridge. The plan was to cross the western ridge flanking the Lairig Ghru, but if the conditions were not favourable then the infamous valley itself was plan B. I parked up in the Old Bridge of Tilt car park, in a lovely little forest which served as the perfect start to get back into the swing of things. Shortly after setting off I spotted some wild garlic, which I only knew about from very recently reading [a book on Scottish wild harvests](https://saraband.net/sb-title/a-handbook-of-scotlands-wild-harvest-2), so I felt very clever identifying and harvesting some for later use.

For five blissful hours I rambled through Glen Tilt, following the River Tilt northwards on a very gentle incline. It was a crisp, sunny morning with plenty of opportunities to stop and rest by burns, waterfalls, and groups of sheep. I tucked into my fully loaded bacon/egg/cheese/salad sandwich - a final fresh food feast before switching to my much sadder ultralight rations for the rest of the journey. This part of the walk was quite busy, and I wondered if I would miss the thrill of sheer isolation I had during last year's Cape Wrath Trail section hike. I did enjoy chatting to the people I met, though; a group of lads bagging a Corbett, a fellow solo traveller [fastpacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fastpacking) a similar route to me but at twice the speed, and an old man with a dog named Rose.

The path eventually forks, with the main route continuing through the glen, and an alternative scrappy-looking branch leading up over a ridge. This was the path leading to the [Tarf Hotel](https://www.mountainbothies.org.uk/bothies/eastern-highlands/tarf-hotel-feith-uaine) - my final destination for the day. It was a bit of a detour (1 hour each way) but I do love bothies, and the promise of a guaranteed dry night to start the expedition off with was too good to pass up. The path was a bit brutal with a cheeky steep incline of 300m, but I broke it up by stopping for lunch near the top. On the menu was chicken super noodles, with some new camping technology I had procured called "coconut oil". This substance, novel to science and mankind, has an insanely high calorie:gram ratio, and also makes everything taste delicious - what a hack! It almost seemed too good to be true, but as we will discover, my culinary hubris would eventually come full circle.

After summiting Mount Tilt (official name [Dun Mor](https://www.hill-bagging.co.uk/hill-view/?rf=4070), I am so amused to learn that every single hill in the UK has been named and mapped by the hill bagging community!), I descended into the neighbouring glen carved out by the Tarf river. The map showed the presence of a ford which could be crossed to get over to the same side of the river as the bothy, but upon inspection it seemed very capable of washing me away, so I consulted the map and found that the river split away upstream, so I opted to walk along the boggy south bank until I found a narrower crossing point. This proved to be a challenge, and in the end my best bet still required me to toss my bag across the rivulet followed by tossing myself across, but I made it! To my absolute delight, I had the whole place to myself.

The bothy (also called Feith Uaine Bothy) was cosy enough, and I found some ancient Scottish relics such as a can of Erwin's lager (circa. 3000 BCE) and a ball of functioning fairy lights. I felt sufficiently remote, so I was very happy with myself. There appeared to be no discernable paths leading to the bothy, which made its large size seem quite strange. It occupies a very dramatic spot surrounded by unpathed hills and flanked by the little River Tarf. It was well worth the detour, and I made myself at home quite easily and might have even dosed off for 40 winks before exploring a little more. The last entry in the book was a month ago in March, and the one before that was January, so it didn't seem to get much traffic.

I settled in for the evening and did some writing, - nothing makes me feel more reflective than a long solo ramble into the wilderness. My thoughts and feelings always tend to be optimistic and positive on these trips. Probably unsurprising given what we know about the benefits of unplugging and immersing into nature. For dinner I scranned a dehydrated camping meal and a handful of Maryland Cookies (elite hiking snack - could potentially be dipped in coconut oil?). I generously left the can of Erwin's for a future visitor.

The heavens opened during the evening and that gave me a smug feeling which elevated the bothy experience even further - knowing that my tent was remaining bone dry and unused for the evening. However, one drawback of the big stone buildings is that they don't trap any body heat like a tiny tent can. The forecast was dipping below freezing overnight, and I could already see my breath as I sat at the desk. After such an early start and a long drive, I ended up going to bed shortly after dusk. I donned my thermals, puffer jacket, hat, and gloves before retreating into my sleeping bag. It was a short hiking day, about 6:30 all in all, but I had a much longer day to follow so an early night and early start made a lot of sense.

## Day 2: Tarf Hotel to Devil's point

  src={tarfRootsImg}
  alt="A slippery fallen root near the Tarf River"

  align="right"
/>

It rained a lot during the night, so it was a damp morning. It was still spitting when I got up just before 07:00. My exposed nose was nithered but otherwise my many layers did their jobs and I woke up feeling warm and well rested. I was keen to get started so I packed up, quickly used the shovel, and then planned my escape back over the stream. The overnight rain had made it swell so I had to scout a bit further along for a better (narrower) crossing, but there were no dramas. The sketchiest part was about an hour later when I was walking along back to the path, I stood on some slippy wet roots that were near-perfectly placed to send me flying into the stream below! Luckily there was a random ledge in that exact spot which I fell onto. The stream wasn't very deep so there was no danger, but it could have spelled a very soggy change of plans. I imagined returning to the bothy cold, wet, broken ankle, etc. and holing up in there for months until help arrived - surviving (comfortably) on rations of coconut oil and maryland cookies.

I returned to the main path and the original "ford" (torrent) and stopped in a small hut for some breakfast (a handful of nuts and some delicious very dark dark chocolate). Climbing the wee hill to get back to Glen Tilt and continue down the main path, the thick morning mist began to lift and I noticed more snow had fallen on the hill tops. I took a moment to scold myself again for not thinking to bring my microspikes, and worried about the disappearing chances of my ridge plan. With that out the way, I took in the stunning views of the glens and the distant snowy mountains. I came across some dramatic drystone ruins and thought about the history of this region, imagining these dwellings being inhabited hundreds of years ago. It is fascinating to imagine, but also tragic to think about the clearances and why some of these places are no longer occupied. To add to the spectacle of the scene, I then spotted a herd of deer on a nearby hillside! A few of them had antlers. It never gets old to see these huge creatures roaming the highlands and running along the hill tops in near silence. My serene main-character moment was interrupted when I was scared absolutely shitless by walking past some hidden grouses (greese?) who flew away suddenly when I walked past.

Rejoining the path, I immediately bumped into a cyclist, which broke the spell of solitude, but he did turn out to be a font of local knowledge. He said he had been in the mountains for 10 days (???). When I nervously asked him about the conditions up there, he described them as "surprisingly wintry" - another nail in the coffin for the ridge plan. I passed the Falls of Tarf, nice vibes, good camping spot, old couple sat around eating scotch eggs, very jealous. I could surely find room in my heart to allow myself to pack some scotch eggs next time, damn the pack weight! The last couple of hours of Glen Tilt were quite uneventful, and I found my mind wandering. I pretended I was the sole survivor of a civilization-ending apocalyptic scenario. I patrolled the glen and shot a distant fellow hiker (zombie) with my trekking pole (hunting rifle).

Eventually the landscape opened up and I could see Red House Bothy in the distance. No idea why they called it that, maybe some Hendrix fans in the MBA, but it seemed nice enough. I had to cross a freezing cold ford to get there; it was very wide but very shallow, so I just carefully removed my boots and rolled up my trousers and tip-toed through to stay dry. When I was about half way across I saw a woman on a bike approach the ford, dismount, and smash through fully clothed pushing her bike and getting soaked. I suppose there is more than one way to skin a haggis.

I popped the head into Red House and met some lads inside preparing lunch who were doing a similar route to me but in reverse. I asked them about the ridge. They hadn't done the ridge but said it looked "very snowy", oh dear. I wanted to make a bit more progress before having my lunch so I left them and continued onwards. I came across a very atmospheric ruin which looked to have been a full on dwelling - with a hearth, window frames, and a combination of drystone and brickwork. I then came across a small patch of trees where I stopped to have lunch. I made more noodles and coconut oil, but this time also added in some of the wild garlic I had found the previous day, delicious! Feeling like the best parts of Ray Mears and Anthony Bourdain combined, I marched boldly onwards for another short while before checking in on my map and realising I had come the wrong way. I knew that I had to turn left onto a minor trail at White Bridge - but I hadn't crossed any white bridges yet. Backtracking a bit, I realised my mistake in the form of naively assuming White Bridge would be white. Perhaps this was karma for my sarcastic Red House comment earlier.

After this navigational blip, it wasn't long before I rounded a corner and saw a truly wonderful sight; the Cairngorms mountains themselves, with Devil's Point front and centre, looking suitably dramatic but also...snow free! At first I wasn't convinced that I was looking at the right thing, but then I remembered its actual Gaelic name ["Bod an Deamhain"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Devil%27s_Point) and concluded that I could not be mistaken. Besides, I had obviously circled it several times already in [MSFS](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Flight_Simulator_(2020_video_game)>) during the hike's R&D phase. This presented a tantalising opportunity. My rough camping plan for the evening had been to try in or around Corrour Bothy, but the lads at Red House had warned that it was both heaving and minging (paraphrasing) so I was already in the market for an alternative. With Devil's Point so close by, and conditions up top looking superb (including a very promising overnight summit forecast with no wind or precipitation), I decided that I would march straight past the bothy and straight up the mountain. I had been dying for ages to do a summit camp, so this was a great opportunity for the bucket list. It also meant I could assess the ridge situation close-up in the morning - and decide to either continue onwards, or head back down the mountain and walk the Lairig Ghru. Having hatched this plan, I hurried onwards to Corrour Bothy and made it in no time.

Just as I had been warned, [Corrour Bothy](https://www.mountainbothies.org.uk/bothies/eastern-highlands/corrour) was very busy with fellow Easter hikers. Clearly the allure of a double-barrelled composting toilet (!) is too compelling to ignore. Who can blame us? Having never been, I imagined this must be exactly what Everest Base Camp looks like during peak season. I stopped for a bit and chatted to the residents, while I gathered my strength for what would be quite a brutal end-of-day 450m vertical ascent to the, um, tip. I have nothing good to say about this part of the hike, which took less than an hour to complete but several months to mentally recover from. I mostly crawled up, doubled over, clinging to my poles. I felt like a turtle with really long front legs. I could feel my blood sugar plummeting with every step, which I deftly compensated for by stuffing my face with more of the very dark dark chocolate. I was encouraged half way up by two kindly women coming down, but I'm sorry to say they didn't get the best version of me. They assured me there were some good pitches in the saddle, but tragically I had no idea what a saddle was.

I finally made it up the brow of the hill, basically on all fours, and saw before me what can only be described as a saddle. To my left was the prominent tip itself, to my right the ridge path stretched off menacingly into the evening, and right in the middle was a pleasant little patch of boulderless meadow. Looking back down the hill, the climb seemed impossibly steep. I could clearly see the bothy and patchwork of tents below me, and I felt very pleased with myself for having made the final push to get away from the crowds. The pitch was impeccable; bouncy grass, slight incline, the works. I was excited to finally camp up a mountain, an experience that had eluded me for far too long. After recovering from the climb, I set to work pitching my tent. The pegs went in like a knife through butter, naturally, and I had views of snowy peaks out of both side doors - spectacular! I nearly cried!

After pitching and basking for a short while, I clambered over the final few boulders to the summit to watch the sun set. It truly is a magical spot, and I felt very privileged to be up there on my own on a still, sunny evening. The view is truly panoramic; Deesside and Lochnager are straight ahead with an uninterrupted view all the way to the horizon; to the left, almost immediately in front of you, is Cairngorm itself and the tallest peak in the area, Ben Macdui; to the right is another smattering of Munroes (1km+ tall mountains are ten-a-penny round here); and then directly, nerve-wrackingly, bum-tinglingly below is the sheer vertical cliff leading to the Glen, where the River Tilt snakes back through it, guiding the eye back towards my starting point. I felt like if I looked hard enough I would be able to spot the wee Yaris waiting for me in the forest. It is quite novel to be able to actually see all the ground that has been covered so far in a multi-day hike, and it really puts things into perspective to know that I had walked beyond the limits of the horizon in just two days. It's also hard not to get into a reflective mood under such conditions, so I sat there peacefully for a while and soaked it all in.
